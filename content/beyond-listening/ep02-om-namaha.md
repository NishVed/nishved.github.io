## Why this song

*Geetanjali* is a Mani Ratnam film from 1989, with a six-song soundtrack by Ilaiyaraaja. Its
premise — two young people, one of them dying, falling in love anyway — will sound familiar to
anyone who has seen *The Fault in Our Stars* or *Dil Bechara*. It got there decades earlier.

*Om Namaha* means to bow down in respect. The song is an ode to the exact moment love is born,
and it's sung by S. P. Balasubrahmanyam and S. Janaki, with lyrics by Veturi Sundara Rama Murthy.

What makes it worth taking apart is that almost everything in it is doing something slightly
unusual, while sounding completely natural.

## Rhythm: a heartbeat that won't sit in four

The rhythm imitates a heartbeat — and it's in **7/8**.

That's the first surprise. Seven is an odd meter; it doesn't divide evenly, and it doesn't
resolve the way four does. It's a grouping you feel as slightly off-balance, perpetually leaning
forward. For a song about the moment love arrives, a pulse that won't quite settle is not an
accident.

It sits at roughly 220 BPM and stays stable throughout, with guitar carrying the accompaniment.

If you want to feel it: count seven evenly, then drop the third beat and change the sound on the
fifth, sixth and seventh. The groove that emerges is the one under the whole song.

## Melody: leaping, not stepping

The song is a duet, and the variation comes from switching between male and female voices across
sections — **call and response**. The melodic phrase itself is simple and repeats; what changes
is who sings it and what harmony sits under it.

The useful distinction here is **conjunct versus disjunct** motion. Conjunct melodies move in
steps — neighbouring notes, easy to sing, the way most nursery tunes work. Disjunct melodies
leap.

*Om Namaha* is full of disjunct motion, which is exactly why the verses are harder to sing than
they sound. The melody keeps jumping. Most listeners register it as graceful; most singers
discover it isn't easy.

## Harmony: a pendulum between tonic and dominant

The song opens on the syllable *Om* — a sound considered in many traditions to be the sound of
the universe, and generally placed around the pitch **E♭**.

The song is in E♭.

From there it runs a **tonic and dominant** relationship, and the structure is almost
mechanically symmetrical. The male voice sings the phrase in the tonic key. The female voice
takes the same melodic shape and shifts it to the dominant, B♭. The structure of the melody
doesn't change — only its harmonic footing does. Then it swings back:

> tonic → dominant → dominant → tonic
> male → female → male → female

A pendulum. Two voices, one shape, alternating ground.

There's also a **B major chord** used as an accidental — raising the fifth from B♭ to B — which
creates a moment of tension against everything around it. One note, deliberately outside the key,
doing a great deal of work.

## Tone colour: a simple arrangement, densely used

The arrangement is interestingly restrained: a main vocal melody, a drum pattern supported by
guitar running throughout, strings in the background, and interludes between verse and chorus.

The interludes are where it gets interesting. Chimes, mandolin, double bass, flute and violins
appear, with a pulsing bass line that imitates the drum groove rather than simply anchoring it.
Instruments **imitate** each other — one states a figure, another answers it.

The second interlude is the clearest case: a conversation between flute and violins, each
answering the other's phrase. That's **counterpoint** — independent lines that remain individually
coherent while fitting together.

## What to listen for

1. **Count seven.** Once you hear the 7/8, you can't unhear the forward lean.
2. **Follow one phrase across voices** and notice the harmonic ground shift under it while the
   shape stays put.
3. **Wait for the second interlude** and listen to the flute and violins take turns.
4. **Find the tension chord** — the one moment that sounds like it stepped outside the key,
   because it did.

## Further listening

*Lingashtakam* sits in a similar harmonic world, and makes a good companion piece if you want to
hear the same tonic-dominant logic in a devotional context rather than a romantic one.
